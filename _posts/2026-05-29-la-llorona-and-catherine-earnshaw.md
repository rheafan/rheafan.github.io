---
title: "La Llorona and Catherine Earnshaw: Nature, Femininity, and “What is a Quiet Earth?”"
layout: post
---
“I lingered around them, under that benign sky; watched the moths fluttering among the heath, and hare-bells; listened to the soft wind breathing through the grass; and wondered how anyone could ever imagine unquiet slumbers, for the sleepers in that quiet earth” (Bronte 334).

	“Chica,” they used to tell me, voices rasping barely above a whisper, “Don’t go by the Rio Grande at night. Actually, you shouldn’t go near the gorge at all in monsoon season.” Monsoon to eight-year-old me meant endless bouts of sunshine rain, torrential downpours that went as quickly as they came. It meant less hours outside, more time spent watching the raindrops race each other down the windowpanes. And the old, superstitious women, bent with age but eyes alight with kindness, meant well in their warning. I hadn’t needed to ask why; they were eager to share. Once, they said, a very beautiful woman found out her Spaniard husband was unfaithful. In a fit of rage, she drowned her own children. But when she realized what she had done, she became consumed by grief, and promptly drowned herself. Now, she is a Hispanic-American folktale, by the name of La Llorona (“the weeping woman”). 
“You can hear her wailing for her babies at night,” they said, “Always searching near rivers, lakes, any source of water.” Both La Llorona and Catherine Earnshaw are women whose earthly passions and losses refuse to be contained by death. Their lives and stories are passed on by the people who live and breathe their natural environments, to whom storytelling and folklore easily become superstition and cautionary tales. The local villagers and shepherds who claim Heathcliff and Catherine’s spirits walk on do so because they brave the harsh, craggy landscape of the moors daily; to them, this earth is certainly not quiet. Similarly, the old women who travel in small groups, a semi-nomadic presence going from place to place selling small artisan goods, walk the desert every breathing moment. They told La Llorona’s story mostly as a cautionary tale to sole male travelers, who were often said to meet unfortunate fates if they heard her cries (some died, others lost their minds, all depending on the version of the story). The character of the male traveler mirrors that of Lockwood. He is an outsider who observes and misreads the female tragedy before him. Both men stand at the precipice of a sorrow they cannot comprehend, evoking somewhat of an inequality between the observer and the observed. These outsiders can watch, standing still for a moment, but then they move on. On the other hand, the female counterparts are unable to move on, they will always be seeking or wandering, unable to rest. As characters, Catherine Earnshaw and La Llorona are inseparable from the aptly named “Mother Nature”. For example, Bronte’s image of “soft wind breathing through the grass” evokes a sense of respiration, as though the earth itself were sighing upon the tragic sight of the three gravestones. In Wuthering Heights, nature does not merely reflect emotion, it becomes it. 
Bronte chooses to conclude Wuthering Heights from the same perspective that began it — the very picture of respectable society: Lockwood. He, as an outsider, does not believe Catherine and Heathcliff continue to roam the moors in the afterlife. Even though Lockwood listens to the tragic stories of the entwined families, he only hears (or chooses to hear) the gentle sounds of springtime. However, Bronte shows that even this callous, emotionally detached man “lingered” by their graves — he does not stand or watch, he “lingers”. This diction depicts how the ever-sensible Lockwood is reluctant, and cannot bring himself to leave. Furthermore, lingering is synonymous with haunting. In this context, haunting is meant without a negative connotation, simply describing the state of being tethered to the earth rather than the heavens in the afterlife. We know that Catherine cannot possibly be at home in heaven, be it metaphorical or literal. She herself said she did not belong in heaven, when living at Thrushcross Grange. Catherine was born and will always be a daughter of the moors, shown by how she was surrounded by lavish things and manmade beauty in Thrushcross Grange, yet only longed for the raw wilderness and freedom of Wuthering Heights. Even Catherine truly believed what she wanted was social status and wealth, things Edgar Linton could provide for her, but time proved her true desire was for her old bed, in Wuthering Heights. She and Heathcliff are inextricably tied to the natural landscape around them, whose dramatic nature reflects the turbulence of the novel. By contrast, Bronte chooses to end the novel on an uncharacteristically quiet note. All the crashing tides of passion and vengeance, incandescent joy and relentless violence, are softened into gentle waves. This could reflect the changed nature of the new generation, Cathy and Hareton, who begin healing from the scars inflicted by their parents on each other and on their offspring. Perhaps this is where the cruelty comes to an end, to a quiet stop put by two people determined to love themselves and each other. Or perhaps it is Bronte’s final criticism of society’s falseness, glossing over the jagged, imperfect beauty of Wuthering Heights and smoothing out its rough edges into a picturesque landscape. 
Bronte’s usage of “quiet earth” depicts peaceful rest, something that has never been granted to all throughout history. The period of colonialism through which La Llorona’s tale arose marked a time when the enslaved, colonized, and indigenous were repeatedly denied the dignity of marked graves. Their rest was forcibly made unquiet. In tandem with the idea that some were not granted peaceful rest due to their origins, Heathcliff’s ambiguous ethnicity points to the idea that peace was always more available to some. He may have become cruel through being continually abused, he may have become callous after Catherine’s rejection of him, he likely even went mad after Catherine’s death. But that does not change how Heathcliff’s very birth made him less susceptible to attaining final peace. His volatile personality and arguably overdependent tendencies display this, especially as he begs Catherine to haunt him when she is on her deathbed. Due to the political and historical implications of the unquiet earth during the time of Spanish colonialism in the Americas, La Llorona’s endless weeping is not only a personal grief, it is the grief of a people. She belongs to a tradition molded by conquest and dispossession, symbolizing the destruction of indigenous family structures. It is her husband, a Spanish colonizer in most versions of the tale, who triggers the tragic chain of events. Being able to sleep finally in a “quiet earth” is a privilege rather than something to be taken for granted, as Lockwood does. Therefore, the unquiet dead may not be vengeful ghosts, but rather, people whom history has wronged.
