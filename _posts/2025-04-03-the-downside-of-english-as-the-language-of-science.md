title: '"Sorry, what do you mean?": the Downside of English as the Language of Science'
---
A foreign language will never be as fluent as a mother tongue. Writing scientific papers that use a great amount of technical language is not only challenging to do in a second language, it can result in crucial disparities between what is written in the paper versus what the writer intends to express. Though English may seem convenient as a universal language in the science world, it poses hindrances to those who learn it as a second language and are pressured to write papers in it -- such as a degree of uncomfortability, lack of precision, and lower work efficiency, all of which must be fairly taken into account by publishers. 

Since the 20th century, English has been predominant in the science world. It is convenient for those born and raised in English to write papers in English, even while using highly technical language, and creates a universality in the science world. However, this is a completely different story for those who do not speak it natively. For nonnative English speakers, it may take a significantly greater amount of time to write a paper, as seen in a survey that resulted in the finding that "preparing a manuscript in English took on average about 12 more working days than if they were writing in Spanish [their native tongue]" (C). The lessening of work efficiency due to a language barrier can hinder those scientists from publishing at the same rates as native English scientists, giving them an advantage. It is easy for the science community to be dominated by native English scientists, who are able to publish great amounts in a lesser period of time, compared to nonnative English scientists. One might ask, "why must these scientists publish in English anyway?" Scientists are pressured to write in English "to be recognized as having internationally compatible quality", as journals in their native tongues may be lesser-known and will not yield as many citations (B). Because English is so dominant, non-English scientists are often forced to go the extra mile and learn a foreign language in order to be published. This is an issue in itself, but it cannot be addressed until nonnative English scientists' language barriers are  taken into consideration by publishers, so as to not give an unfair advantage to those that speak English natively. The primary focus must be on the content and findings of the paper, and less on how fluently it is presented.

It is true that the relative versatility of the English language allows it to be a common medium across a multitude of academic fields. The use of English as a common tool can actually increase the dispersion of knowledge throughout the world, simply because so many people speak and use it. In fact, English is the most commonly learned second language across schools in non-English speaking countries. However, it remains particularly difficult for native speakers of languages not in the Germanic family, like Semitic and Sinitic speakers, as they have vastly different grammatical structures and even writing systems -- Semitic is often based off of diacritics, and Sinitic relies upon a logographic system. This goes to show that native speakers of languages such as Spanish and French may have relatively less difficulty picking up English as they at least share the same alphabet, while native speakers of Arabic and Chinese have to learn an entirely new writing system as well. There are certain errors specific to native speakers of certain languages, which must be taken into account by academic institutions and publishers. If publishers do not have knowledge of common errors, they may turn down valuable papers that simply have a lack of language proficiency. For example, because Chinese has no articles, it is easy for native Chinese speakers to leave out "the" and "a" in their writing -- a simple error, but one that can cause a paper to be disregarded, or even rejected from publication. Because "when meanings are complex and technical, precision is a must", the way a paper communicates its findings is crucial, and if it cannot communicate well due to the writer's level of English, academic institutions should be responsible for aiding the writer (A). It must be made clear that the paper is insufficient not because its findings are not valuable, but because the way said findings are communicated is faulty. This is not in any way the writer's "fault", as learning English is something additional to being proficient in their science. It is up to the institution to provide support for those who need it, in order to level an already uneven playing field. However, translation services remain the most expensive out of all editing services in 2020, presenting an economic barrier to non-English scientists looking to be published (D). Until translation becomes widespread and commonplace for non-English scientists looking to be published (instead of an additional cost and inconvenience), the science world will largely remain without a diverse range of opinions from people of different languages and backgrounds.
